## Claim, reason, example, evidence

### Reading `TXT-B2-01-L01-001` — Should city centres restrict private cars?
Supporters argue that reducing private-car access can make city centres quieter, safer and more attractive for pedestrians. They also point out that less traffic can improve bus reliability. However, restrictions may affect people who live in areas with weak public transport or who need a car for work or mobility reasons. A policy that begins with better bus and rail connections may therefore be fairer than an immediate ban. The strongest case is not that cars are always harmful, but that city transport should offer realistic alternatives before access is restricted.

## Counterargument and concession

Although… / While it is true that… / Admittedly… / however… / Critics may argue that… / yet…

Rule: represent the opposing position fairly enough that its supporter would recognise it.

Bad: “People who want office work hate flexibility.”  
Better: “Supporters of more office time may accept flexibility but argue that some collaborative tasks work better face to face.”

## Evaluate support

Strength ladder:
1. unsupported assertion;
2. personal example;
3. relevant descriptive information;
4. stronger evidence from a credible source/method appropriate to the claim.

> **SIMULATED TRAINING SOURCE — not real research.** A company survey of 480 employees found that most respondents preferred two or three remote days per week, but the survey did not measure productivity.

### Reading `TXT-B2-01-EXT-001` — Should hybrid work become the default?
Debates about remote and office work are often presented as a choice between freedom and collaboration, but the reality is more complicated. Employees who work from home may save commuting time and find it easier to concentrate on tasks that require sustained individual attention. For some people, greater control over the working day can also improve access to employment. At the same time, fully remote arrangements may make informal communication, onboarding and certain kinds of joint problem-solving more difficult.

This does not mean that office work is automatically more productive. A busy office can create interruptions, and travelling to a workplace has costs in both time and money. Nor does it follow that every employee values flexibility in the same way. Job role, home environment, team structure and personal circumstances all affect what works well.

A more defensible policy may therefore be to define which activities require shared presence and which can be completed independently. A hybrid system is likely to work best when expectations are explicit: teams need agreed availability, reliable communication channels and a clear reason for asking people to come in. The important question is not simply where people work, but whether the working arrangement matches the task and the needs of the team.

Tasks: identify the writer’s thesis; find two concessions; distinguish examples from general claims; write one fair counterargument; identify the qualification in the conclusion.

### Listening `AUD-B2-01-EXT-001`
> I used to think a fixed office schedule was the simplest solution because everyone knew when colleagues would be available. I still see that advantage, especially for new staff, but I’ve changed my position to some extent. Our team now works from home two days a week, and the arrangement has been successful mainly because we are quite specific about what happens on office days. We use those days for planning, difficult conversations and collaborative work. Individual analysis and routine writing are often done at home. That doesn’t prove the same model would suit every organisation, but it has made me less convinced by arguments that treat remote and office work as complete opposites.

## Benchmark contrasts for the writing task
### Too absolute / weak
> Remote work is better because people are happier and offices are unnecessary. Everyone should work from home.


### Underdeveloped B2
> Working from home has advantages and disadvantages. It saves time, but people may miss colleagues. I think hybrid work is best.

Strength: balanced direction. Weakness: points are not developed or supported.

### Strong B2 model
> A hybrid arrangement is likely to be the most practical option for many teams because it combines flexibility with regular opportunities for face-to-face collaboration. Working from home can reduce commuting time and may help with individual tasks that require concentration. However, supporters of more office time reasonably point out that new staff and complex group decisions can benefit from direct contact. For this reason, I would not recommend a fixed number of remote days for every department. Instead, teams should identify which activities genuinely require shared presence and agree on clear availability rules. This approach is not guaranteed to solve every coordination problem, but it is more adaptable than treating remote and office work as complete opposites.


## B2/C1 rubric boundary
B2 requires sustained argument, relevant support, fair counterpoint and clear qualification. It does **not** require routine multi-source synthesis, subtle implicit stance across several texts or sophisticated register transformation; those are C1 territory.

## Language models
- may/might/tends to: qualify claim
  Remote work may help concentration.
- Although / Admittedly / While: concede
  Admittedly, coordination can be harder.
- Critics may argue that: represent counterargument
  Critics may argue that…
- This does not mean / Nor does it follow: block over-inference
  This does not mean office work is always better.
- therefore / nevertheless: link argument
  Therefore, teams should define shared presence.
