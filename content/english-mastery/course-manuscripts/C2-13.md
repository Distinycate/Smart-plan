## Perspectives, interests and hidden tensions

Scenario: automated writing feedback.

**Teacher**
> Immediate feedback could help students revise more often, but automated suggestions should not be treated as unquestionably correct. Students need to understand that feedback can be useful without being authoritative.

**Administrator**
> A scalable option is needed because teachers cannot provide detailed comments on every draft. If the system handles routine feedback, staff could focus on higher-value guidance.

**Student representative**
> Fast feedback is useful, but students should be able to see whether feedback came from a teacher or a system. They should also have a clear way to question feedback that does not make sense.

Tasks: common ground? **fast/useful feedback has potential** · teacher concern? **authority/reliability** · administrator priority? **scale/resources** · student priority? **transparency/challenge path** · unresolved issue? **system authority/accountability**

## Preserve nuance while reformulating

Source: `The proposal is unlikely to eliminate the problem.`  
Distorted: `The proposal will not work.`  
Faithful: `The proposal may reduce the problem, but it is not expected to solve it completely.`

Precision network: reduce ≠ eliminate · evidence ≠ proof · concern ≠ objection · possible ≠ probable · efficient ≠ effective · consensus ≠ absence of disagreement.

## Specialist ↔ public transformation

### Simulated specialist source
> The intervention produced a statistically detectable improvement in short-term recall, but effect sizes were heterogeneous and diminished at delayed follow-up. The findings therefore support a limited claim about immediate performance rather than a general claim about durable learning.

### Public mediation
> The intervention improved short-term memory on average, but the size of the benefit varied and became smaller when people were tested later. So the results suggest an immediate benefit, not clear proof of a lasting learning effect.

Preserved: improvement, variation, smaller delayed effect, limited conclusion.  
Removed: specialist terminology not needed by the audience.  
Not allowed: “The intervention improves learning.” — scope and certainty broadened.

### Source D — quality-assurance note (simulated)
A review of automated feedback found that the system was highly consistent when identifying a limited set of surface-level issues, but substantially less predictable when comments depended on intended tone, rhetorical purpose or discipline-specific conventions. The reviewers did not conclude that such feedback was unusable. Instead, they recommended differentiating between low-risk suggestions that could be presented directly and higher-interpretation suggestions that should be framed as prompts for consideration. They also argued that users should be able to see why a suggestion was generated and should not be encouraged to assume that consistency is equivalent to correctness.

### Source E — teacher working-group statement (simulated)
Teachers supported faster feedback in principle but disagreed about what role it should play. Some wanted the system to handle routine corrections so that staff could focus on argument, organisation and individual guidance. Others worried that students might accept automated comments uncritically, particularly when the feedback sounded confident. The group’s strongest point of agreement was therefore not about the amount of automation, but about the need for transparent labelling, opportunities to question feedback and clear responsibility for final academic judgement.

### Source F — student representative audio (simulated)
> From a student perspective, speed matters, but confidence can be misleading. If a message appears instantly and sounds certain, people may assume it has been checked by a teacher even when it hasn’t. I don’t think the answer is to remove automated feedback. I think the system should make its role obvious: tell us when a comment is automated, show us when the system is uncertain, and give us a simple route to ask a person when the advice seems inconsistent with the task. That would make the feedback easier to trust precisely because it would be clearer about its limits.

### High-level mediation task

3. a 120-word public explanation that preserves the distinction between consistency, correctness and appropriate human oversight;
4. a two-minute spoken intervention that acknowledges face concerns and proposes the next evidence-gathering step.

## Two defensible mediation openings
### Opening A — process-first
> There appears to be agreement that automated feedback can be useful for routine issues, while interpretive feedback carries a different level of risk. The unresolved question is how selective human escalation can be without making the system either misleading or operationally pointless. A reasonable next step would be a limited pilot that separates direct corrections from advisory comments, labels automated feedback clearly and records which cases are escalated.

### Opening B — stakeholder-first
> The participants are protecting different but compatible priorities: teachers are concerned about academic judgement, operations about scalability, and students about transparency and recourse. Rather than deciding immediately between automation and human feedback, the group could test a narrower model in which routine comments are automated, higher-interpretation comments are explicitly advisory, and disputed cases can be referred to staff.


## Compression-loss case
Original:
> The review found a small average improvement, concentrated in routine grammar tasks, with inconsistent performance on tone and rhetorical purpose; the authors therefore recommended limited use with clear escalation for interpretive feedback.

Bad compression:
> The review found that automated feedback improved writing and recommended using it.

Loss: task scope narrowed to “writing” generally; limitations removed; recommendation strengthened.

Faithful compression:
> The review found modest benefits for routine language feedback but inconsistent results for interpretation, so it recommended limited use with escalation safeguards.
