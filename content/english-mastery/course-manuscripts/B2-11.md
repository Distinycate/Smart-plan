Reading A
An editor reviews a student's report and changes 'do a decision' to 'make a decision.' The problem is not that the reader cannot guess the meaning; the combination is unconventional in this context. Elsewhere, the student writes 'strong rain,' where 'heavy rain' is the usual phrase. The editor asks the student to record useful combinations rather than isolated translations. Word families also matter: a reliable source provides reliability, but adding a suffix mechanically can produce a word that does not fit. Learning phraseology involves meaning, grammatical pattern and register, not simply replacing every ordinary word with a longer one.

Read for purpose, then locate the people, events, conditions and limits. Map claim, supporting evidence, alternative explanation and limitation. Compare sources on the same question. Reading B

## Listen, clarify and interact

Listening script B
Compare a dictionary example with your intended meaning. Check the grammatical pattern and the audience. Keep a record of corrections, but do not label every unfamiliar expression wrong without considering dialect, genre or specialised usage.

First pass: identify purpose and main message. Second pass: note essential details and any corrections. Check against the script only after the listening attempt when approved audio becomes available. For now, practise with a teacher or partner reading the script.

Pronunciation and prosody
Use prominence to distinguish your position from a reported view. Pause at changes of argument. Signal concession and contrast with phrasing, without relying on exaggerated stress to make weak evidence sound stronger.

Interaction rehearsal: take both roles. Ask one clarification question, answer it using the source, and check that the other person has understood. Do not equate quietness, accent or hesitation with lack of ability.
