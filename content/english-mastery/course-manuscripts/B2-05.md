Reading A
A fictional town is moving several public services online. Supporters argue that digital forms save travel time and allow applications outside office hours. A community group welcomes that flexibility but points out that some residents share devices, have limited connectivity or need assistance reading forms. The disagreement is not simply between progress and resistance. It concerns how access is defined and who bears the cost of adapting. A phased introduction with staffed alternatives may address some problems, although it requires resources. Evaluating the change means considering both convenience for confident users and the experience of people who might otherwise disappear from the usage statistics.

Read for purpose, then locate the people, events, conditions and limits. Map claim, supporting evidence, alternative explanation and limitation. Compare sources on the same question. Build a proportionate conclusion and address a fair counterargument. Explain why your evidence supports a claim rather than merely repeating it.

Reading B
Consultation summary: Forty people responded online; eight attended a drop-in session. Several online respondents wanted faster processing. Drop-in participants asked for clear instructions and a way to correct mistakes. These groups were self-selected and cannot automatically represent all residents.

Compare the two inputs: what is shared, what is additional, and what is not known? Use only details actually supplied. All characters, organisations, figures and events in these inputs are fictional classroom materials.

Listening script B
Identify benefits, burdens and missing voices. Distinguish criticism of implementation from rejection of the goal. When reporting consultation results, explain how people took part and who may have been excluded by the method.

First pass: identify purpose and main message. Second pass: note essential details and any corrections. Check against the script only after the listening attempt when approved audio becomes available. For now, practise with a teacher or partner reading the script.

Pronunciation and prosody
Use prominence to distinguish your position from a reported view. Pause at changes of argument. Signal concession and contrast with phrasing, without relying on exaggerated stress to make weak evidence sound stronger.

Interaction rehearsal: take both roles. Ask one clarification question, answer it using the source, and check that the other person has understood. Do not equate quietness, accent or hesitation with lack of ability.
