# C1-08 — Synthesising Multiple Sources

Version 0.6.0 · PRE_HUMAN_CONTENT_COMPLETE · Preserved pilot derivative; human review pending.

## เป้าหมาย
Identify significant claims; distinguish overlap/difference/complementarity; paraphrase without stance drift; synthesise by theme; adapt output to audience; mediate complex information for non-specialists.

## Select what matters

Capture: central claim · relevant support · limitation · relation to other sources · audience relevance.

### Source A — Staff flexibility survey (simulated)
A medium-sized organisation introduced flexible start and finish times for six months. Staff surveys reported improved satisfaction, especially among employees with family responsibilities. Managers observed fewer late arrivals. Some teams, however, found cross-department meetings harder to schedule. Productivity was not measured. The survey therefore supports claims about reported satisfaction and some attendance patterns, but not claims that flexibility increased productivity.

### Source B — Coordination analysis (simulated)
Autonomy can support job satisfaction, but coordination costs may rise when teams depend heavily on synchronous collaboration. Flexible schedules tend to work better when organisations define shared availability windows, communication expectations and decision responsibilities. The analysis does not argue that one timetable is suitable for all teams; rather, it suggests that flexibility and coordination mechanisms need to be designed together.

### Source C — Manager interview (simulated spoken source)
> Flexibility itself isn’t the problem. The difficulty comes when everyone assumes they can choose completely different hours without telling the team. Once we introduced two common collaboration periods each day, most of the complaints disappeared. People still had freedom outside those periods, so we didn’t need to return to fixed schedules.

Tasks: claim supported by ≥2 sources? **flexibility works better with coordination rules/common availability** · A-only limitation? **productivity not measured** · C relation to B? **practical mechanism/example** · unsupported claim? **flexibility definitely raises productivity**

## Paraphrase without semantic drift

Original: “Productivity was not measured.”  
Bad: “Productivity did not improve.”  

Original: “Flexible schedules tend to work better when…”  
Bad: “Flexible schedules only work when…”  

Fidelity test: factual meaning? certainty? logical relation? source ownership?

## Compare and merge

Avoid: `Source A says… Source B says… Source C says…`


| Theme | A | B | C |
|---|---|---|---|
| satisfaction/autonomy | higher reported satisfaction | autonomy may support satisfaction | not central |
| coordination | meetings harder | coordination costs may rise | complaints when hours uncoordinated |
| response | not tested | shared windows/norms | two common periods helped |

## Evaluate limitations

C1 does not require formal research-method expertise. Notice limitations relevant to meaning:
- A cannot support productivity claims;
- C is one manager’s account and illustrates a mechanism rather than universal proof;
- the sources broadly converge on coordination but differ in source type/evidential strength.

### Source D — internal implementation review (simulated)
After nine months of flexible scheduling, the organisation reported that staff satisfaction remained higher than before the change, while the number of late arrivals stayed lower. The review nevertheless found considerable variation between teams. Units with predictable individual workloads adapted quickly, whereas teams that relied on frequent cross-department decisions reported delays when colleagues’ working hours overlapped less. The review concluded that the policy itself was neither clearly successful nor unsuccessful in isolation. Outcomes appeared to depend on whether managers established shared availability periods, response-time expectations and clear ownership of time-sensitive decisions. The document also noted that the review did not compare the organisation with a similar company using fixed hours, so it could not determine how much of the change was caused by the scheduling policy alone.

### Source E — employee forum contribution (simulated)
> The flexibility has made my working day much easier, but the first few months were confusing because “flexible” meant something different in every team. Once our department agreed that everyone would be reachable between ten and three, the system became much easier to manage. I would not want to return to fixed hours, although I can understand why teams with constant customer contact might need tighter rules than ours.

### Source F — audio policy commentary (simulated)
> The interesting point is that flexibility and coordination are sometimes treated as opposites, when in practice they can support each other. A flexible policy without shared rules may create uncertainty; very rigid rules may remove the benefits that employees value. The more useful question is which elements need to be common and which can remain individual. From the evidence we have here, shared collaboration windows seem promising, but the sources are not strong enough to establish one universal model. They tell us more about conditions for implementation than about a single best timetable.

## Language models
- reported stance verbs: attribute source position
  Source A reports…, Source B suggests…
- Taken together: integrate sources
  Taken together, the sources suggest…
- whereas / while: contrast source claims
  A foregrounds satisfaction, whereas B foregrounds coordination.
- caution frames: preserve qualification
  The evidence should be interpreted cautiously.
- audience reformulation: change density, not truth
  For a director, foreground the implementation condition.
